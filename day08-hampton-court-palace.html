Day 08: Hampton Court Palace

December 3, 2023
Scroll Down

Today (Sunday) we are off to the Fayre Festival at Hampton Court Palace. It was 5 degrees at 9 a.m. We were going to get the 720 bus to Earlsfield Station and then the train to Hampton Court. Our ticket to the Palace is for between 11.30 and 11.59 a.m. The bus was not coming for another 17 minutes. More importantly, the train we were going to get had been cancelled. Pete ordered an Uber. A driver accepted but then cancelled. Another driver accepted and arrived fairly quickly. Pete sat in the front after a necessary seat adjustment. On the way, Amber fogged up the window with her heavy breathing1. A good drive with more interesting scenery. There were many stalls at the Palace selling a variety of items. It was cloudy with some light showers but we soon moved inside so the weather wasn’t of immediate concern.

The original Tudor Hampton Court Palace was begun by Cardinal Wolsey in the early 16th century but it soon attracted the attention of Henry VIII who subsequently brought all of his six wives there. Hampton Court Palace was built for 200,000 crowns (about £57.6M). Today it is estimated to be worth about 1B.

King Henry VIII’s Great Hall was very impressive with its magnificent stained glass windows, towering ceilings and huge tapestries adorning the walls.

The Palace was much bigger than we thought. There were lovely paintings (mainly portraits) and tapestries everywhere2. There were paintings on many of the ceilings like the Sistene Chapel. Amber and Pete had a game of Fox and the Geese (a quite difficult game to master). There were lots of doors leading off everywhere but closed to the public. I wonder where they all lead. It must have been a hive of activity during King Henry VIII’s reign with all the servants running hither and thither to cater to the King’s needs but at the same time trying to avoid his wrath for some minor misdemeanor.

We had toasties (2 x cheese and bacon and 1 x fungi) for lunch with diet cokes. There was light rain and very little shelter but the toasties were delicious.

We saw the Indian Army at the Palace display (including a video presentation). The Indian Army camped at Hampton Court Palace on a number of occasions in the early 20th century. A large contingent of Indians attended the coronation of 1902, 1911, 1935 and 1937 and the Peace Parade of 1919.

Next we visited the Chapel Court Garden and Henry’s wine cellar and kitchens.

William III’s apartments were very impressive and had a spectacular view of the Privy Gardens. The apartments were rebuilt for William III by Christopher Wren. Since the reign of King George II no royal has resided at Hampton Court. The Guard Chamber of the apartments displays a remarkable collection of weapons. Yeoman of the Guard would have been stationed at the door, checking courtiers were suitably dressed and appropriately behaved before allowing them into the Presence Chamber3. This is the official throne room, with the chair of estate under its official canopy. Visitors still had to bow to the throne even when it was empty.

We had a walk around the Privy Gardens. Very spectacular.

We went for a walk amongst the stalls down to the canal to view the long water and the deer grazing (there are about 300 in total) and used to be hunted by King Henry and the other royals.

One duck was very upset that Amber had nothing to feed it.

Unfortunately, the maze wasn’t open to the public, but we did get to walk through some more gardens.

As we were leaving it started to rain. We were pretty much soaked by the time we got on the train to Earlsfield. After alighting the train we ran for the bus to Tooting. It was still raining. We were lucky to get seats upstairs. We arrived home at 5 p.m. We had toasties for tea (leftover spaghetti and meatballs and pastrami, cheese and tomato). Very delicious. Amber made another cocktail. They are getting better all the time.

Watched Unchartered on Netflix while we had tea.

Some fireworks activity in the park despite the inclement weather. There is a leak in the roof that is dripping onto the armrest of the lounge.

The date on this painting ‘ANNO 1580’ appears to be original. The inscription ‘QUEEN ELIZABETHS PORTER’ may have been added later. It is not known who the sitter was or whether he was indeed a porter; his fine clothes might imply a higher station. His features show characteristics of gigantism as does the actual size of the painted image (eight feet tall). A scale is provided for the viewer by the staff he holds in his right hand, which is in fact a musket rest, something which comes up to eye level for a normal man.

4 - Knyff was born in The Hague and had settled in England by 1681; he specialised in bird’s-eye views of English country houses, recording their architecture and agriculture with great attention to detail. In a letter of 9 January 1703 Knyff wrote, ‘I have done a great many [drawings] of Hampton Courte and Windsor for his Majesty [Queen Anne’s husband, Prince George of Denmark] which are not yet engraved’. This painting was not acquired for the Royal Collection until 1948 and no engraving of this composition survives, but in the absence of contradictory evidence it may be connected with these drawings referred to in 1703.

This date fits with the topography of the Palace, showing the transformation wrought here by William III. Taking the view from the east (the rear of the palace) emphasises the Baroque parts at the expense of the Tudor. We see Wren’s Fountain Court and new garden facades (south and east) as well as the formal gardens created by George London and Henry Wise to go with them. William III’s Banqueting House appears near the river and the enclosed rectangular gardens to the north of the Palace all show the changes he made in these areas.

On the Thames are barges and a ferry transporting a coach; on the other side of the river is the village of East Molesey, and Hampton can be seen in the distance.
